# Validation — first pass on real data

Generated by `pipeline/.venv/bin/python -m pipeline.validate` (rule engine from `backend/app`).
This is a first, automatic check, not a clinical validation.

## 1. False alarms on healthy people (LifeSnaps, real Fitbit days + EMA mood)

Participants with ≥ 70 resting-HR days: **23**. Personal passport = first 40 recorded days; assessed days after that: **1276** (14-day rolling history, mission day ≥ 30 so no adaptation window).

| Overall level | Days | Share |
|---|---|---|
| GREEN | 1175 | 92.1% |
| YELLOW | 82 | 6.4% |
| ORANGE | 19 | 1.5% |
| RED | 0 | 0.0% |

Non-GREEN on healthy days: **7.9%**.

Rules that fired (rule · level · hypothesis · days):

| Rule | Level | Hypothesis | Days |
|---|---|---|---|
| R-SLP-10 | YELLOW | Late insomnia | 61 |
| R-SLP-01 | YELLOW | Sleep deficit | 32 |
| R-BEH-01 | ORANGE | Depression | 19 |
| R-BEH-10 | YELLOW | Depression | 4 |
| R-CV-17 | YELLOW | Fatigue impairing performance | 3 |

Reading: on these healthy volunteers 0 day(s) reached RED and 19 reached ORANGE. Sleep rules fired on 93 rule-days: real people often sleep < 6 h, or poorly on most nights of a fortnight; that matches NASA's own threshold (< 6 h = restricted sleep), so it works as a self-care nudge rather than a medical alarm. Mood rules fired on 23 rule-days. LifeSnaps has no 1-7 mood scale: we derive one from EMA labels (a day tagged only TENSE/ANXIOUS or SAD maps to 1/7), which is harsher than a real rating, so these alarms say more about the mapping than about R-BEH-01. A real pre-flight mood check-in is needed before judging that rule. The personal-baseline HRV rule (R-CV-17) fires rarely.

**Finding:** 4 of 28 real draws from healthy astronauts trigger the glucose rules (R-NUT-02 54–69 mg/dL → YELLOW, R-NUT-01 < 54 → RED). These are serum values from routine draws, not symptomatic hypoglycemia. The ADA bands are written for people on glucose-lowering treatment; for healthy crew the rule should probably require symptoms (or compare with the personal baseline). Flagged for review by a flight surgeon; the rule is unchanged for now. The demo flight crew carries no glucose readings (no blood is drawn in flight), so the demo is not affected.

**Excluded as suspected sample handling (3 draws):** 3 of the four crew read 36–44 mg/dL glucose at the same draw (R+45) with a normal anion gap and enzymes, and one of them also reads potassium 5.9 mmol/L there. Low glucose with high potassium is the pattern of serum left on the cells too long (glycolysis, potassium leak). The values stay in the warehouse as published, with `quality = suspect` (`pipeline/inspiration4.py`), and are not fed to the rules.

Reading: from vitals alone the engine escalates only patients with abnormal vital signs; most urgent ED patients are urgent because of symptoms (chest pain, stroke signs, trauma), which the crew would enter through the symptom check-in. The demo has almost no non-urgent stays, so it cannot measure false alarms; the LifeSnaps section above covers that side.

## 4. Glucose bands on continuous glucose monitoring (BIG IDEAs Lab, Dexcom G6)

16 adults without diabetes (HbA1c 5.2–6.4%), 36,898 CGM readings over 150 participant-days.

- readings < 70 mg/dL (R-NUT-02 band): **0.6%**; readings < 54 mg/dL (R-NUT-01): **0.1%**
- days with at least one reading < 70 mg/dL: **23 of 150 (15%)**; people with such a day: **8 of 16**

Reading: low readings are rare (0.6% of all readings) but appear on 15% of days, so a rule that fires on any single value below 70 mg/dL would alarm about once a week per crew member on a CGM. Together with the Inspiration4 draws above, this supports gating R-NUT-01/02 on symptoms or on repeated low readings before using them with continuous monitors in flight.

## 5. Orthostatic criteria on healthy controls (PhysioNet head-up tilt, 55–75 y)

20 controls: median systolic change on tilt -0.9 mmHg, median HR change +12.1 bpm. Meet the hypotension criterion (SBP ≤ −20 or DBP ≤ −10): **0**; the tachycardia criterion (HR ≥ +30): **0**.

Reading: the knowledge-base criteria stay quiet on healthy controls, so a positive test on landing day is meaningful (NASA reports 66% of ISS crew failing a 10-minute tilt on landing day).

## 7. Balance baseline (NHANES 2003–2004 modified Romberg, adults 30–55)

927 adults: pass all 4 conditions **71%**; pass foam + eyes closed (the vestibular condition) **71%**.

Reading: 29% of healthy adults fail the foam/eyes-closed condition on Earth, so a single pre-flight fail is not a red flag; what matters is the change against the crew member's own pre-flight result after landing.

## 6. Biodosimetry from blood gene expression (NASA OSDR OSD-410 / GEO GSE63952)

100 samples from 10 volunteers (ex vivo gamma rays, 0 / 0.15 / 0.3 / 1.5 Gy, 1–6 h). Panel of 20 radiation-responsive genes, fold change vs the same person's unexposed sample, nearest-centroid classifier, leave-one-volunteer-out.

- accuracy for none / low (0.15–0.3 Gy) / high (1.5 Gy): **64%** (chance 33%)
- strongest genes: AEN, CDKN1A, TRIAP1, GADD45A, PCNA, DDB2, RPS27L, FDXR
- confusion (true->predicted: n): high->high: 15, high->low: 8, high->none: 2, low->high: 16, low->low: 24, low->none: 10, none->none: 25

Reading: unexposed samples are recognised in 25 of 25; exposure (0.15–1.5 Gy) is detected in 63 of 75 (84%), but the dose level is often wrong (high recognised as high in 15 of 25). As a lab-on-chip it can say 'exposed, check the dosimeter', not give a dose; a better dose estimate needs a time-aware model or more genes. Model: `data/warehouse/biodosimetry_model.json`.
